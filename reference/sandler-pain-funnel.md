# Sandler Pain Funnel — Reference

The pain funnel is Sandler's core diagnostic sequence. It moves a prospect from surface-level symptoms to emotionally owned pain. Do not skip levels. Do not move to budget or proposal until pain is owned.

---

## The Funnel (Top to Bottom)

### Level 1: Surface Pain
**What they say:** General frustration or problem statement.
**What you do:** Acknowledge, then ask for specifics.

> "Tell me more about that."
> "What does that look like in practice?"
> "When did this start?"

### Level 2: Specifics
**What you need:** Concrete description of the problem — not feelings, but facts.
**What you do:** Probe for frequency, scope, who's affected.

> "How often does this happen?"
> "Who else is dealing with this?"
> "What have you tried to fix it?"

### Level 3: Failed Solutions
**Why it matters:** Understanding what's been tried establishes why your approach is different. Also reveals how long they've been living with the problem.
**What you do:** Acknowledge the effort, don't dismiss past vendors.

> "What happened when you tried that?"
> "What worked about it? What didn't?"
> "What made you stop?"

### Level 4: Emotional Cost
**Why it matters:** Intellectual pain doesn't move deals. Emotional pain does.
**What you do:** Ask how the problem affects them personally.

> "How is this affecting you personally?"
> "What does it feel like to be dealing with this every week?"
> "What does it mean for your team when this isn't solved?"

### Level 5: Dollar Cost
**Why it matters:** Creates a reference point for ROI and budget.
**What you do:** Ask for the number — don't supply it.

> "If you had to put a number on what this is costing you — revenue lost, time, resources — what would that be?"
> "What does it cost you when a lead converts at 2% versus 5%?"
> "If this was solved a year ago, what would be different?"

### Level 6: Pain Ownership
**The signal:** The prospect names the cost in their own words, without prompting.
**Why it matters:** Pain they named is pain they own. Pain you named is pain you can't anchor to.
**What you do:** Reflect it back.

> "So if I heard you right, this is costing you roughly $X and has been going on for about Y months — and that's primarily landing on you and [team]. Did I get that right?"

## Sandler Four-Frame Deal Check

Run this before every proposal recommendation. Flag any unknown.

| Dimension | What to confirm | Risk if unknown |
|---|---|---|
| **Pain** | Is the dollar/emotional cost named by the prospect (not you)? | Proposal anchor has no reference. Price becomes the story. |
| **Budget** | Is there money attached to solving this problem? | Proposal gets killed by sticker shock. |
| **Decision** | Who signs? Timeline? Who has hidden veto? | Deal stalls after proposal. |
| **Control** | Is the next step specific — date, time, deliverable? | Deal goes quiet. Prospect "goes dark." |

## Common Sandler Mistakes (Diagnose These in Transcripts)

**Unpacking the trunk too early**
Selling features before pain is owned. The prospect hasn't said "this costs me X" but you're already explaining your methodology.
*Fix: Stop. Ask a Level 4 or 5 pain question before continuing.*

**Rescuing**
Sensing discomfort and immediately softening your position or offering a discount.
*Fix: Hold the frame. Silence is not rejection.*

**Going for the close before budget is qualified**
Proposing a $6,000/month retainer to someone who hasn't confirmed they have money attached to this problem.
*Fix: Ask the budget frame question before building the proposal.*

**Vague next step**
"I'll send over some information" is not a next step. It has no date, no commitment, and no response expected.
*Fix: "Can we schedule 30 minutes Thursday at 2pm to walk through what I put together?" — specific date, time, and deliverable.*

**Agreeing with every objection**
"You're right, it is a big investment." This confirms the objection instead of exploring it.
*Fix: "What would make that investment feel justified to you?"*
